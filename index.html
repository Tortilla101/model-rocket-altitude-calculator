About Cd: A smooth single-diameter rocket with clean paint typically runs Cd 0.5 to 0.75. A rocket with a diameter step or shoulder (like a payload section wider than the booster) can run considerably higher. Flow separation at the step adds real drag that a simple frontal-area formula doesn't capture. The most reliable way to get an accurate Cd is either (a) a proper simulation of your exact geometry in OpenRocket or RockSim, or (b) flight-calibration: fly the rocket once with a tracked or altimeter-recorded altitude, then adjust Cd here until the calculator matches that real result. Once calibrated for a specific rocket, that Cd is valid for future predictions with that same rocket as long as the build doesn't change.

How this calculator works (for team leads and mentors)
Simulation: the flight is simulated in small time steps from liftoff through apogee. Each step updates velocity from (thrust minus drag minus gravity) divided by mass, in two dimensions (vertical and horizontal), and updates mass as propellant burns off. Thrust direction is set by an effective tilt angle equal to rail angle plus estimated weathercocking.

Weathercocking estimate: approximated once, using rail-exit speed, as arctan(wind speed divided by rail-exit speed), then held constant through the flight. This is a simplification. Real weathercocking also depends on the rocket's static stability margin (how many calibers of stability it has), which this calculator does not model directly. Rockets with a low stability margin will weathercock more than this estimates. Very stable rockets will weathercock less.

Air density: computed from temperature, humidity, and pressure using the moist-air density equation, not just the standard-atmosphere default most calculators use.

Dynamic Cd and induced drag: Cd is allowed to rise with speed toward a build-style-dependent ceiling. This is a calibration heuristic representing real-world effects like tape lift or seam separation at high dynamic pressure, not a measured aerodynamic curve. At these motors' speeds (well below Mach 0.3), true compressibility drag rise is not a real factor yet, so treat this as a tunable knob, not a physics law. Crosswind-induced angle of attack adds extra drag using safe geometry that stays well-behaved at zero velocity, with a capped multiplier to keep the simulation numerically stable.

Known limitations: assumes a flat, simplified thrust curve unless the F39T preset is selected. Does not model fin cant, surface roughness in detail, or recovery deployment. The weathercocking model is approximate and does not use stability margin. For competition-grade precision, cross-check against a real tracked or altimeter flight and calibrate Cd as described above.
